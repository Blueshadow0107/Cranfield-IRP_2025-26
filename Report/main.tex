%% --- ABSTRACT --- %%
\begin{abstract}
This thesis characterises a light-sensitive Belousov--Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction--diffusion medium as a structured molecular-communication substrate.
The dynamics are modelled by the two-variable Tyson--Fife Oregonator with a spatial light-suppression field $\phi(x,y)$.
A verified explicit finite-difference solver is used in a black-box protocol to measure native input--output transfer functions: pulse transmission along channels, refractory-limited frequency response, two-input collision logic, and anisotropic routing.
Measurements show a near-ideal channel wire, a maximum 1:1 following rate of 0.167 per time unit under the dark-spot drive, an inhibition gate A~AND~(NOT~B) with separation ratios above $200\times$, and anisotropic patch delays that vary continuously with fibre angle.
Polynomial-chaos/Sobol sensitivity analysis quantifies how uncertainty in the kinetic parameters propagates into pulse speed and amplitude, giving the characterisation a robustness layer.
A trainable light-field T-junction demonstrates that a static $\phi(x,y)$ can route single pulses, while a band-pass frequency-discrimination task returns a negative training result that marks the practical limit of simple light-field modulation.
The work provides a component-level characterisation of the substrate---the prerequisite for designing trained chemical processors---and identifies physics-informed parameter inference and reduced-order modelling as the next steps toward closed-loop design.
\end{abstract}
