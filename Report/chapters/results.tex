\section{Solver Verification}
\label{sec:res_verification}

Before any transfer function was trusted, the solver of \refSec{sec:meth_solver} was verified by the method of manufactured solutions and by grid and timestep convergence studies.
In the manufactured-solution test, an analytic source term is added to the kinetics so that a known field solves the equations exactly, and the discrete error is measured as the grid and the time step are refined.
Three configurations were tested: Barkley kinetics with isotropic diffusion, Barkley kinetics with a tilted anisotropic tensor, and Oregonator kinetics at candidate A.
\reffig{fig:res_mms} shows the measured error norms against grid spacing and time step.
The observed spatial convergence orders at the finest refinement interval are $2.09$, $2.09$, and $1.99$ for the three cases, against a design order of $2$ for the central-difference diffusion operator.
The observed temporal orders are approximately $1.0$ across all cases, matching the first-order operator splitting of \refSec{sec:meth_discretisation}.
The tilted-tensor case converges at the same rate as the isotropic case, validating the divergence-form tensor discretisation that the anisotropy experiment of \refSec{sec:res_anisotropy} depends on.

Convergence of the physical quantity of interest, the plane-pulse speed, is summarised in \reftab{tab:res_convergence} and \reffig{fig:res_verification}.
The Barkley speed rises monotonically from $4.151$ cells per time unit at $\Delta x = 1$ to $4.603$ at $\Delta x = 0.25$; a Richardson extrapolation gives a continuum asymptote of approximately $4.82$, so the working resolution underestimates the Barkley speed by about $14\%$.
The observed order of the sequence is only about $0.8$: the refinement is still pre-asymptotic, because the pulse front is only two to three cells thick at these resolutions and is not yet smooth on the grid.
The Oregonator candidate-A speeds, $6.224$, $6.048$, and $6.049$ cells per time unit across the same refinement, lie within a $2.8\%$ band, close to grid-converged at the working resolution.
Timestep refinement changes measured speeds and timings by at most $1.5\%$ for both kinetics at the working step $\Delta t = 0.05$, and a joint $(\Delta x, \Delta t)$ refinement map shows the error to be space-dominated and approximately separable: at fixed $\Delta x$, temporal refinement contributes less than $0.7\%$ further change.

\section{Channel Pulse Transfer}
\label{sec:res_channel}

The first component characterised is the wire.
In the free medium, a plane pulse travels at $4.151$ cells per time unit with the Barkley kinetics and at $6.223$ cells per time unit with the Oregonator at candidate A.
\reffig{fig:res_channel} shows the measured transfer of a single pulse along a straight walled channel as the channel width $W$ is swept down to a single cell.
The result is exact and somewhat surprising: the channel speed is bit-identical to the free-medium speed at every width tested, down to $W = 1$ cell, and the pulse transmits with undiminished amplitude.
No geometric wave block was observed at any width; the eikonal critical width below which confinement would extinguish the pulse is sub-grid for this medium.

The physics of this result is clean.
A pulse travelling in a straight channel is a planar front, and a planar front has zero curvature, so the eikonal curvature correction to its speed vanishes \cite{keener1991eikonal}.
The no-flux walls impose a zero normal derivative that a planar front already satisfies identically, so the walls change nothing: propagation in the channel is exactly equivalent to propagation on a one-dimensional cable.
The width-induced block documented in ring and cable studies \cite{courtemanche1993instabilities} requires the front to acquire curvature, which a straight uniform channel never forces on it.
As a device characteristic the result cuts both ways: a straight channel is a near-perfect wire whose speed and amplitude are set by the kinetics alone, but its width offers no computing leverage.
Structure that computes must come from junctions, curvature, or the property fields, not from confinement alone.

The clamped-port result was cross-checked with the experimentally honest dark-spot drive of \refSec{sec:meth_darkspot}: a circular patch at the channel entrance is dimmed to $\phi = 0.002$ for $3.0$ time units, emitting a single pulse into the channel.
The dark-spot free-medium speed is $6.46$ cells per time unit and the channel speed is $6.46$--$6.47$ cells per time unit at every width from $W = 60$ down to $W = 1$, so the width-independence of the wire is reproduced.
The absolute speed is about $4\%$ higher than the clamped-port value ($6.22$ cells per time unit), an honest difference between the two input encodings; the important point is that neither drive shows a geometric wave block.

\section{Frequency Response}
\label{sec:res_frequency}

The second experiment measures the substrate's bandwidth, which is set by refractoriness.
The two-pulse measurement locates the absolute refractory period directly: $2.80$ time units for the Barkley kinetics, $3.10$ time units for the Oregonator at candidate A, and $2.70$ time units at candidate B.
Sustained pulse trains then give the transmission curves of \reffig{fig:res_frequency_barkley} and \reffig{fig:res_frequency_oregonator}.
At low input frequency the output frequency follows the input one-to-one: the curve has unit slope and every pulse transmits.
The maximum 1:1 following rate is $0.263$ per time unit for the Barkley kinetics and $0.282$ per time unit for the Oregonator at candidate A, rising to $0.323$ per time unit at candidate B.
Above this rate the curve leaves the unit slope and develops a staircase of dropped pulses, in which the medium locks to rational transmission ratios as more input pulses arrive inside the refractory wake of their predecessors.

Two features of the measurement deserve note.
First, the maximum 1:1 following rate lies below the naive estimate given by the inverse of the absolute refractory period: in a sustained train, each pulse propagates into the only partially recovered wake of the previous one, travels more slowly, and so extends the effective refractory interval, a rate dependence familiar from measured transfer functions of excitable cables \cite{delange2009transfer}.
Second, the computational reading is direct: refractoriness is the substrate's clock limit.
Any signal encoding must space pulses by more than the refractory time, and any cascade of devices must operate below the maximum following rate.
The same property is also a native filter: the channel low-passes pulse trains for free, the same mechanism the junction literature exploits for frequency selection \cite{gorecka2003tshaped}.

The frequency measurement was repeated with the dark-spot drive to test whether the experimentally honest input encoding can reproduce the intrinsic refractory bandwidth.
A single circular spot at the channel entrance was flashed to $\phi = 0.002$ for $3.0$ time units; because the flash must end before the next flash can begin, the start-to-start period cannot be shorter than the flash itself.
\reffig{fig:res_frequency_darkspot} shows the result: the maximum 1:1 following rate is $0.167$ per time unit, well below the clamped-port Oregonator value of $0.282$ per time unit.
The shortfall is not a failure of the medium --- the Oregonator refractory period is unchanged --- but a property of the input encoder: a single dark spot flashed for $3.0$ time units cannot be asked to emit a sustained train with period shorter than about $6.0$ time units without the flashes merging into one long pacemaker flash.
For pulse-train signalling this is a real constraint; for single-event computation it is irrelevant.

\section{Two-Input Collision Logic}
\label{sec:res_logic}

The third experiment measures the substrate's native logic at a T-junction, with one input channel per arm and a probe on the output branch.
The first finding is negative and important: read naively over the whole record, the junction computes OR, whatever it was designed to do - the unconditional diffraction background identified in the protocol design of \refSec{sec:meth_protocol}.
For the same reason, the complementary construction, annihilation XOR, is not realised by the passive junction alone: diffracted copies of a lone pulse reach the output probe whether or not the other input fires.
This is consistent with the collision-computing literature, where working gates rely on precisely timed inputs and purpose-built geometry rather than on an unadorned junction \cite{toth1995logic, steinbock1996chemical, adamatzky2002xor}.

With the windowed readout of \refSec{sec:meth_protocol}, the same junction implements a genuine two-input gate.
The probe record is read only within a window aligned to the expected arrival of a lone pulse from the designated through-arm, input A.
If input B fires within the coincidence interval, its pulse collides with the A pulse at the junction, the two fronts annihilate, and the window reads silent.
\reftab{tab:res_truth} gives the measured truth table: the windowed device computes the inhibition gate A AND (NOT B), with B acting as a veto on A.
\reffig{fig:res_logic} and \reffig{fig:res_logic_snapshots} show the underlying probe records and wave fields.

Three figures of merit quantify the gate.
The separation ratio, the weakest true output against the strongest false one, is approximately $1000\times$ for the Barkley kinetics, a value capped by the probe noise floor rather than by the physics, and $232\times$ and $276\times$ for the Oregonator at candidates A and B.
The inhibition window, the range of relative input timings within which the trailing pulse is still annihilated, spans $2.0$ to $4.0$ time units.
The window also yields the design rule for scaling such gates: the difference in input-arm lengths must be engineered against the distance a pulse covers within the window, $c$ times $2.0$ to $4.0$ cells, since an asymmetry beyond that budget lets the veto pulse arrive too late to collide.
The device should therefore be understood as timing-gated rather than as pure combinational logic: its truth table holds only for inputs synchronised within the measured window, and the readout window is part of the device.

\section{Anisotropic Substrate}
\label{sec:res_anisotropy}

The fourth experiment characterises the anisotropic parameterisation of \refSec{sec:meth_anisotropy}.
In a uniform tensor field with anisotropy ratio $r = D_{\parallel}/D_{\perp}$, the expanding front is expected to follow the $\sqrt{r}$ eikonal law of \refSec{sec:meth_anisotropy}.
The measured front geometry follows this law closely, as \reffig{fig:res_aniso} shows; the isotropic control $r = 1$ reproduces the circular front exactly.
At $r = 2$ the deviation from the $\sqrt{r}$ prediction is $4.6\%$ for the Barkley kinetics and $0.24\%$ for the Oregonator; at $r = 4$ it is $9.6\%$ and $0.34\%$ respectively; and at $r = 8$ the Barkley deviation reaches $10.2\%$, still within the $\approx 14\%$ spatial error bar established for that kinetics in \refSec{sec:res_verification}.
The full directional speed profile $c(\theta)$, probed along rays at several orientations to the fibre direction, matches the elliptic theory to within $1.2\%$.
The tensor discretisation is therefore quantitatively trustworthy; the residual Barkley deviations are accounted for by the known, openly stated grid error rather than by any failure of the anisotropic formulation.

The device-relevant measurement embeds a patch of anisotropic medium in the isotropic substrate and records the delay a pulse accumulates crossing it.
As the fibre direction of the patch is rotated from parallel ($0$ degrees) to perpendicular ($90$ degrees) to the incident propagation direction, the transmission delay grows monotonically from $644$ to $836$ time steps.
The response is smooth, monotone, and continuous in the fibre angle: the patch acts as a continuously tunable delay element, and two patches with different orientations route pulses along paths of different effective length without ever blocking them.
This steers propagation in the interior of the medium rather than at its boundaries, a routing capability neither walls nor the light field provides, in the spirit of patterned-excitability experiments \cite{steinbock1995anisotropy}.

The anisotropic $\sqrt{r}$ check was also attempted with the dark-spot stimulus, but the result was not usable: a circular dark patch of radius 12 cells at $\phi = 0.002$ acts as a pacemaker for the duration of the flash and, in the uniform anisotropic field, continues to emit successive target waves that interfere before the first front can be cleanly tracked.
The measured front extents are therefore multi-valued and the extracted ratio $c_{\parallel}/c_{\perp}$ does not follow the $\sqrt{r}$ law.
The clamped-port seed result reported above remains the validated measurement of the tensor discretisation; the dark-spot drive is better suited to channel-like geometries where the flash duration has been calibrated for a single emitted pulse.

\section{Parameter Window for the Light Field}
\label{sec:res_phi_window}

The trainable control in this work is the illumination field $\phi(x,y)$, so its usable range must be known before any optimisation is attempted.
\reffig{fig:phi_regime_map} combines a linear-stability scan of the homogeneous rest state with sparse channel-propagation tests at $f = 1.4375$, $\varepsilon = 0.0501$.
Three regimes appear.
For $\phi \lesssim 0.0045$ the rest state is unstable and the medium oscillates spontaneously.
For $\phi \gtrsim 0.0045$ the rest state is stable and the point kinetics are excitable, but only up to $\phi \approx 0.026$ does a launched pulse actually propagate tens of cells.
Above $\phi \approx 0.028$ the medium is excitable in the local sense yet too suppressed to sustain a spatial pulse.
The working point $\phi = 0.010$ sits near the centre of the usable window, equation~\refeqn{eq:phi_window}.

This window shapes what training can realistically do.
Raising $\phi$ can slow, attenuate, or locally block waves; lowering it can let waves through or, if taken too far, make the medium oscillatory.
The dynamic range between `pass' and `block' is therefore bounded, and the transition is sharp rather than gradual.
Any trained field must keep most of the medium inside the green window of \reffig{fig:phi_regime_map}, which limits how strongly $\phi$ can modulate propagation and makes routing by $\phi$ alone a harder optimisation problem than a continuous `attenuation' picture would suggest.

\section{Uncertainty Quantification of the Channel Pulse}
\label{sec:res_uq}

The four kinetic parameters that define the Oregonator working point are not known exactly in any real preparation, so the channel transfer function was subjected to a local one-at-a-time sensitivity sweep.
Each of $\varepsilon$, $f$, $\phi$ and $D_{u}$ was varied independently by $\pm 10\%$ around its nominal value while the other three were held fixed, giving nine additional channel-pulse solves.
The quantities of interest were the pulse speed $c$ between the near and far probes and the peak activator $u_{\mathrm{peak}}$ at the far probe.

\reffig{fig:res_uq_sensitivity} summarises the normalised changes.
For pulse speed, the largest response comes from $\varepsilon$ ($\approx -6\%$ for $+10\%$ change, $\approx +7\%$ for $-10\%$ change) and from $D_{u}$ ($\approx +5\%$/$-5\%$), while $f$ and $\phi$ produce only $\approx \pm 2\%$ changes.
For the probe peak, the dominant parameter is $f$ ($\approx \pm 4\%$), followed by $\varepsilon$ ($\approx \pm 3\%$); $\phi$ and $D_{u}$ have only a weak effect on amplitude ($\lesssim 1\%$).
All runs remained in the stable-excitable regime and produced finite pulses.

The design message is that timing budgets are most sensitive to the activator/recovery timescale $\varepsilon$ and the activator diffusivity $D_{u}$: a $10\%$ uncertainty in either produces a comparable speed uncertainty.
The stoichiometric factor $f$ is more important for pulse amplitude than for speed at this operating point, while the background illumination $\phi$ has a relatively mild influence inside the chosen $\pm 10\%$ band.

To move beyond the local OAT picture, a variance-based Sobol decomposition was run on the same four parameters using a Saltelli sample of $N=64$ ($384$ forward solves, all successful).
\reffig{fig:res_uq_sobol} shows the first-order and total-effect indices.
For pulse speed, the first-order indices are dominated by $\varepsilon$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.59$), followed by $D_{u}$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.32$), $\phi$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.11$) and $f$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.08$).
For the probe peak, the ranking is different: $f$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.65$) and $\varepsilon$ ($S_{1}\approx 0.37$) carry almost all the variance, while $\phi$ and $D_{u}$ are essentially inactive.
The sum of first-order speed indices is close to one, indicating weak interactions; for the peak, $f$ and $\varepsilon$ together exceed one, suggesting a small negative interaction between them.
The key message is that speed and amplitude must be calibrated against different parameters, and that $\phi$ is not the dominant uncertainty source inside the $\pm 10\%$ band around the working point.

\section{T-Junction Routing by Trained Illumination}
\label{sec:res_tjunc}

As a first training target, the T-junction directionality task of \refSec{sec:meth_tjunc_task} was constructed with fixed walls and a trainable illumination field $\phi(x,y)$ around the junction.
The geometry is a vertical stem feeding a horizontal bar; an input dark spot at the base of the stem launches a single pulse, and the objective is to make the pulse reach the left output probe while remaining below threshold at the right probe (and vice versa).
Probes record the spatial maximum of $u$ over a small disk, because the mean over the disk is diluted by the wake and underestimates the wavefront amplitude at a T-junction.

Hand-designed fields confirm that $\phi$ can steer the pulse.
With a uniform field both output probes fire, as the incoming wave splits symmetrically.
Raising $\phi$ to the propagation-blocking level ($\approx 0.04$) on one side of the junction suppresses that branch and leaves the other branch free, giving clean single-output routing: a right-side block routes the input to the left probe, and a left-side block routes it to the right probe (\reffig{fig:res_tjunc_hand}).
A high-``phi'' block placed across the top of the junction blocks neither branch effectively, showing that the field must be positioned relative to the incoming wave path, not simply increased everywhere.

This establishes the physical feasibility of trainable routing, but also its limitation: a static $\phi$ field can break the symmetry of a single input, yet it cannot, on its own, route two different inputs entering the same stem to two different outputs, because the field sees both inputs identically.
The trained routing experiment therefore targets the single-input directionality task.

\subsection{Optimiser comparison and trained field}
\label{sec:res_tjunc_train}

The single-input directionality task was used to compare four black-box optimisers: random search, differential evolution (DE), dual annealing, and CMA-ES.
The design space is a $4 \times 4$ bilinear interpolation of $\phi$ on the junction region, giving 16 continuous parameters in $[0.010, 0.040]$.
Each candidate evaluation is one full forward solve of the T-junction.
The soft objective penalises low peak at the assigned output and high peak at the wrong output; the hard objective records whether the assigned probe crosses the $u = 0.5$ threshold while the wrong probe does not.

\reffig{fig:res_optimizer_compare} shows the best soft loss found by each optimiser as a function of the number of expensive forward evaluations.
With a small budget of 20 evaluations, CMA-ES reaches a soft loss of $0.006$ and a hard loss of $0.000$; DE also succeeds, finding a zero-hard-loss field on its first evaluation, while random search and dual annealing remain stuck near a hard loss of $1.000$.
The difference is not merely the algorithm: the landscape is a staircase with wide flat regions, and CMA-ES's covariance learning is better able to discover the correlated block pattern that suppresses one branch without extinguishing the pulse.
The best trained field is shown in \reffig{fig:res_tjunc_trained}: a smooth gradient of high $\phi$ on the right side of the junction routes the input pulse to the left probe (peak $\approx 0.7$, above threshold) while the right probe remains below threshold.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:res_discussion}

Read together, the four experiments constitute a component datasheet for the substrate: a slow, parallel, threshold-logic medium.
Straight channels are near-ideal wires whose speed is set by the kinetics alone, though their width carries no computing leverage (\refSec{sec:res_channel}).
Refractoriness imposes a hard bandwidth ceiling, the medium's clock limit, and doubles as a native pulse-train filter (\refSec{sec:res_frequency}).
Junctions with windowed readout supply native inhibition logic, A AND (NOT B), from which arbitrary Boolean functions can in principle be composed, provided inputs are synchronised within the measured timing window (\refSec{sec:res_logic}).
Anisotropy adds continuous, non-blocking routing and delay control in the interior of the medium (\refSec{sec:res_anisotropy}).
Reproduced across both kinetics (\refSec{sec:meth_overview}), these transfer functions are read as properties of excitable media generally, not of one model's parameterisation.

In the physical-neural-network framing of \refSec{sec:lit_pnn}, the characterisation defines what training would buy.
The native transfer functions are fixed by the kinetics; the three substrate parameterisations of \refSec{sec:meth_parameterisation} reshape them.
Walls are the coarse, binary program: they place the ports, wires, and junctions, but contribute no graded behaviour of their own.
The light field $\phi(x,y)$ is the graded and experimentally rewritable knob \cite{sakurai2002design}; the measurements reported here expose exactly the levers a training loop would pull: regime stability, propagation speed, timing, and block.
The anisotropic tensor is a continuous steering field with a verified quantitative law.
Closing the loop from this characterisation to actual training over $\phi(x,y)$ and the geometry was out of scope for the present work; the limitations of the study and the route to trained substrates are taken up in \refChap{chap:conclusions}.
