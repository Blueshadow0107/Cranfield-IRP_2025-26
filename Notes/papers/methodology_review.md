### 1. Roshko (1954) — NACA Report 1191
**Full title:** *On the Development of Turbulent Wakes from Vortex Streets*  
**Authors:** Anatol Roshko  
**Venue:** NACA Report 1191, California Institute of Technology

**Main point:**  
Wake development behind circular cylinders divides into two distinct Reynolds-number regimes: a **stable range** (Re = 40–150) where regular vortex streets form without turbulence, and an **irregular range** (Re = 150–20,000) where turbulent fluctuations accompany periodic vortex formation. The transition is initiated by laminar-turbulent transition in the free shear layers.

**Methodology:**
- **Experiment:** Low-speed wind tunnel with circular cylinders
- **Measurement:** Standard hot-wire anemometry for velocity fluctuations
- **Range:** Re = 40 to 20,000
- **Analysis:** Spectrum and statistical measurements of velocity fluctuations; study of spanwise structure

**Key findings:**
- Vortices decay by viscous diffusion in the stable range
- In the irregular range, diffusion becomes turbulent and the wake becomes fully turbulent 40–50 diameters downstream
- The vortex street has a periodic spanwise structure in the stable range
- Shedding frequency can be used to measure flow velocity

**Relevance to thesis:** Foundational empirical reference for the stable vortex regime (Re ≈ 40) that Goto et al. later identified as optimal for reservoir computing. Establishes the physical reality of the twin-vortex state before the Hopf bifurcation.

### 2. Williamson & Brown (1998) — J. Fluids Struct.
**Full title:** *A Series in 1/√Re to Represent the Strouhal–Reynolds Number Relationship of the Cylinder Wake*  
**Authors:** C. H. K. Williamson & G. L. Brown  
**Venue:** Journal of Fluids and Structures, 12, 1073–1085

**Main point:**  
The Strouhal–Reynolds (St–Re) relationship for cylinder wakes is better represented by an expansion in **1/√Re** using an effective wake width, rather than traditional polynomial fits. This formulation collapses data over Re = 50–140,000 with dramatically lower error.

**Methodology:**
- **Data synthesis:** Collates experimental shedding-frequency data across a wide Re range
- **Formulation:** Defines effective wake width incorporating body diameter + shear-layer width
- **Fitting:** Truncated series S = A − B/√Re + C/Re + ... compared against Henderson's numerical data up to Re = 1000
- **Validation:** Two-term fit S = 0.2698 − 1.0271/√Re has one order of magnitude less error than traditional three-term fits

**Key findings:**
- St∞ = 1/A ≈ 0.21 is constant independent of geometry in the asymptotic limit (Re → ∞)
- The 1/√Re formulation is physically motivated and fits both laminar and turbulent regimes
- Henderson's 2D numerical data validates the relationship up to Re = 1000

**Relevance to thesis:** Core empirical reference for the sigmoid-like St–Re relationship. The saturation of St at high Re and the smooth transition through the twin-vortex regime is the physical basis for treating cylinder wakes as a trainable nonlinear activation function.

### 3. Kim (2017) — arXiv:1708.04333
**Full title:** *A Unified Strouhal–Reynolds Number Relationship for Laminar Vortex Streets Generated by Different Shaped Obstacles*  
**Authors:** Ildoo Kim & X. L. Wu  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint

**Main point:**  
The St = 1/(A + B/Re) relationship derived for circular cylinders generalises to **arbitrary obstacle shapes** (triangular, square, hexagonal, etc.). Only B is shape-dependent; A (and thus St∞) is universal.

**Methodology:**
- **Experiment:** Freely flowing soap-film tunnel (quasi-2D, suppresses 3D turbulence)
- **Obstacles:** Rods with different cross-sections but aspect ratios ≈ 1
- **Measurement:** Direct visualisation of vortex street wavelength λ vs. obstacle diameter D
- **Key observation:** λ = λ₀ + αD is linear across the entire Re range (10–3×10³)
- **Derivation:** Uses constant c = vst/U ≈ 0.8 and f = vst/λ to derive St = 1/(A + B/Re)

**Key findings:**
- St∞ = 1/A ≈ 0.21 is constant for all tested shapes
- B is the only shape-dependent parameter
- Soap-film experiments enable laminar vortex street study across a broad Re range inaccessible in 3D flows

**Relevance to thesis:** Generalises the St–Re sigmoid to non-circular geometries, supporting the idea that an array of variable-diameter cylinders (or other shapes) can act as a trainable neural network where geometry = weight.

### 4. Morast (2019) — MSc Thesis
**Full title:** *Learning From the von Kármán Vortex Street*  
**Author:** Anthony Morast  
**Venue:** South Dakota School of Mines and Technology, M.S. in Computational Sciences and Robotics

**Main point:**  
Deep learning algorithms can **infer the Reynolds number** parameterising Navier–Stokes flow and **predict unseen object locations** from vortex street wake structure alone, demonstrating that wake patterns encode sufficient information for inverse problems.

**Methodology:**
- **Simulation:** Navier–Stokes CFD (ANSYS Fluent or similar) for cylinder wakes at various Re
- **Task 1 — Re inference:** Multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on wake velocity-field images to classify/predict Re
- **Task 2 — Object detection:** Two cylinders placed upstream of an observer; CNNs predict (x,y) coordinates from downstream wake data alone
- **Data preparation:** Structured grid extraction, normalisation, train/test splitting
- **Software:** Python with TensorFlow/Keras

**Key findings:**
- CNNs successfully learn to parameterise NS equations from wake structure
- High accuracy in predicting object locations from wake data — the wake acts as an information-rich "fingerprint"
- Demonstrates that vortex street structure contains enough information for supervised learning tasks

**Relevance to thesis:** Shows that the vortex street wake can be read out by neural networks — analogous to how photoelastic stress visualization could read out the "output" of a vortex-street neural network. Also validates that different Re regimes produce distinguishable wake signatures.

### 5. Goto et al. (2021) — New J. Phys.
**Full title:** *Twin vortex computer in fluid flow*  
**Authors:** Ken Goto, Kohei Nakajima et al.  
**Venue:** New Journal of Physics, 23, 063051

**Main point:**  
The **twin-vortex state** (just before the Hopf bifurcation at Re ≈ 40–47) is the optimal operating regime for fluid-based reservoir computing. Input sensitivity increases as Re approaches the bifurcation, but echo-state properties break down after the transition to Kármán vortex shedding.

**Methodology:**
- **Simulation:** Computational fluid dynamics (2D incompressible Navier–Stokes) for flow past a circular cylinder
- **Reservoir computing framework:** Cylinder wake = reservoir; input = perturbation to inflow; readout = linear regression on velocity field features
- **Tasks:** NARMA (nonlinear autoregressive moving average) time-series prediction, memory capacity evaluation
- **Parameter sweep:** Re varied across stable, twin-vortex, and Kármán regimes
- **Metric:** Normalised mean square error (NMSE) vs. Re

**Key findings:**
- Peak computational performance occurs near the critical Re (twin-vortex regime), not in the fully developed Kármán street
- Input sensitivity increases monotonically as Re approaches the Hopf bifurcation
- After bifurcation, the system's memory fades too quickly (echo-state property lost)
- The twin-vortex computer is a physical reservoir computer with no external electronic components

**Relevance to thesis:** This is the **seminal paper** for the prof's vortex-NN direction. It establishes that cylinder wakes can compute, identifies the critical operating regime (Re ≈ 40), and provides the reservoir-computing framework for interpreting vortex streets as neural networks.

### 6. Hughes et al. (2019) — Sci. Adv.
**Full title:** *Wave Physics as an Analog Recurrent Neural Network*  
**Authors:** Tyler W. Hughes, Ian A. D. Williamson, Momchil Minkov, Shanhui Fan  
**Venue:** Science Advances

**Main point:**  
Wave-based physical systems (acoustics, optics) are **mathematically isomorphic to recurrent neural networks (RNNs)**. The time dynamics of wave propagation naturally provide recurrence, and the spatial field distribution provides the hidden state. This enables training physical wave systems for temporal signal processing without ADC conversion.

**Methodology:**
- **Theoretical mapping:** Derives equivalence between scalar wave equation discretised in space/time and RNN state-update equations
- **Inverse design:** Uses adjoint-method optimisation to design inhomogeneous medium geometry (refractive index distribution)
- **Training:** Standard backpropagation through time (BPTT) applied to the wave system surrogate
- **Demonstration:** 2D acoustic medium trained for vowel classification on raw audio waveforms
- **Numerical solver:** Finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) equivalent for wave propagation

**Key findings:**
- Exact mathematical mapping: wave field amplitudes at spatial grid points = RNN hidden states; scattering matrix = weight matrix
- Trained medium achieves vowel classification performance comparable to digital RNNs
- Processing occurs in the native analog domain — no digitisation required
- Energy efficiency gains of several orders of magnitude over electronic implementations

**Relevance to thesis:** The theoretical foundation for the **acoustic wave computing** strand of the thesis. Shows that continuous wave propagation *is* a recurrent neural network, providing the mathematical bridge between PDE physics and neural computation.

### 7. Wright et al. (2022) — Nature
**Full title:** *Deep physical neural networks trained with backpropagation*  
**Authors:** Logan G. Wright, Tatsuhiro Onodera, Martin M. Stein, Tianyu Wang, Darren T. Schachter, Zoey Hu, Peter L. McMahon  
**Venue:** Nature, 601, 549–555

**Main point:**  
Introduces **physics-aware training (PAT)** — a hybrid in situ–in silico algorithm that applies backpropagation to train *any* controllable physical system as a neural network, even when the physics lacks mathematical isomorphism to standard ANN layers.

**Methodology:**
- **Core algorithm:** Forward pass = physical system; backward pass = differentiable digital surrogate model; gradient descent on controllable physical parameters
- **Three experimental platforms:**
  1. **Optical:** Diffractive deep neural network (spatial light modulator + free-space propagation) for image classification
  2. **Mechanical:** Elastic metamaterial (3D-printed lattice with tunable stiffness) for vowel recognition
  3. **Electronic:** Analog circuit (variable resistors) for MNIST classification
- **Training:** Standard backpropagation via automatic differentiation; physical parameters updated after each epoch
- **Noise handling:** In situ training automatically compensates for fabrication defects, temperature drift, and noise

**Key findings:**
- Universality: PAT works across optics, mechanics, and electronics
- Physical neural networks achieve comparable accuracy to digital equivalents
- In situ training mitigates imperfections that would break pre-trained digital models
- Potential for orders-of-magnitude speed and energy improvements over digital processors

**Relevance to thesis:** The **canonical reference** for training physical systems. Connects to both the wave-computing strand (optical PNN) and the vortex-street strand (mechanical PNN demonstrates that non-electronic physical systems can be trained). The energy-efficiency argument directly supports the thesis motivation.

### 8. Stern et al. (2020) — PNAS
**Full title:** *Supervised learning through physical changes in a mechanical system*  
**Authors:** Menachem Stern, Chukwunonso Arinze, Leron Perez, Stephanie E. Palmer, Arvind Murugan  
**Venue:** PNAS, 117(26), 14843–14850

**Main point:**  
Mechanical metamaterials can **physically learn** desired force–response behaviours by experiencing training examples. Thin creased sheets learn to classify force patterns through local plastic deformation of crease stiffnesses, generalising to unseen test forces.

**Methodology:**
- **Physical system:** Thin polyethylene sheets with laser-cut crease patterns (origami-inspired metamaterial)
- **Learning rule:** Local plastic deformation — crease stiffness changes in proportion to experienced strain (Hebbian-like physical learning)
- **Training:** Repeated application of training forces; no external computation during training
- **Tasks:** Binary and multi-class force-pattern classification (e.g., localised vs. distributed forces)
- **Measurement:** Force–displacement curves; generalisation error on test forces
- **Comparison:** Training/test error vs. sheet size (model complexity) analogous to bias–variance tradeoff in ML

**Key findings:**
- Sheets learn to classify force patterns with ~90% accuracy
- Generalisation performance follows learning-theory predictions (U-shaped test error vs. model complexity)
- Learning is purely physical — no computer involved in the adaptation process
- Rugged energy landscape of disordered materials is sculpted by local physical learning

**Relevance to thesis:** Demonstrates that **physical materials themselves** can learn without digital computation. The local learning rule (strain-proportional plasticity) is analogous to how a vortex-street NN could adapt cylinder diameters based on local flow measurements.

### 9. Stern et al. (2021) — Phys. Rev. X
**Full title:** *Supervised Learning in Physical Networks: From Machine Learning to Learning Machines*  
**Authors:** Menachem Stern, Daniel Hexner, Jason W. Rocks, Andrea J. Liu  
**Venue:** Physical Review X, 11, 021045

**Main point:**  
Derives **local physically plausible learning rules** (coupled learning / equilibrium propagation) for any physical network, proving that learning requires only local responses and no explicit knowledge of the desired function. Demonstrates in disordered flow networks and elastic networks.

**Methodology:**
- **Theoretical framework:** Coupled learning — a contrastive learning scheme where the network is run twice (free phase + clamped phase) and local updates approximate the gradient of a global cost function
- **Proof:** Shows that local learning rules can be derived for any physical network in equilibrium or steady state
- **Simulations:**
  1. **Disordered flow networks:** Pipe networks with variable conductances; trained for allostery (localised response to localised force)
  2. **Elastic networks:** Spring networks with variable rest lengths; trained for shape-morphing tasks
- **Training:** Local update rule: Δkᵢ ∝ (responseᵢ^clamped − responseᵢ^free) × inputᵢ

**Key findings:**
- Local rules converge to the same solution as global backpropagation for convex problems
- Learning is decentralised — each element updates based only on its own local state
- The framework applies to any physical network regardless of topology or physics type
- Scales to large networks without central processor bottleneck

**Relevance to thesis:** Provides the **theoretical foundation for decentralised physical learning**. The local update rules could apply to a vortex-street network where each cylinder adjusts its diameter based on local pressure/velocity measurements, without global knowledge of the desired output.

### 10. Altman et al. (2023/2024) — arXiv:2311.00170
**Full title:** *Experimental Demonstration of Coupled Learning in Elastic Networks*  
**Authors:** Lauren E. Altman, Menachem Stern, Andrea J. Liu, Douglas J. Durian  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint (accepted Phys. Rev. Applied)

**Main point:**  
First **experimental laboratory demonstration** of coupled learning in mechanical networks. Three proof-of-concept elastic networks of increasing complexity learn tasks (self-symmetrization, node allostery) by iteratively tuning individual spring rest lengths.

**Methodology:**
- **Physical system:** Mechanical networks of springs and rigid bars
- **Learning mechanism:** Iterative adjustment of individual spring rest lengths using local strain measurements
- **Twin-network implementation:** Two identical networks — one free-running, one with clamped outputs; local comparison of responses drives updates
- **Tasks:**
  1. Self-symmetrization: network learns symmetric response from asymmetric inputs
  2. Node allostery: force at one node produces desired displacement at another
- **Equipment:** Force actuators, displacement sensors, microcontroller for local comparison

**Key findings:**
- Coupled learning converges experimentally in real mechanical systems
- No central processor or external memory required — learning is fully decentralised
- Networks can be scaled and automated for increasingly complex tasks
- Demonstrates resilience to noise and component variability

**Relevance to thesis:** The **experimental validation** of Stern et al.'s coupled learning theory. Shows that local physical learning rules work in practice, not just in simulation. Directly applicable to the design of adaptive vortex-street networks.

### 11. Li & Mao (2024) — arXiv:2404.15471
**Full title:** *Training all-mechanical neural networks for task learning through in situ backpropagation*  
**Authors:** Shuaifeng Li & Xiaoming Mao  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint

**Main point:**  
Extends in situ backpropagation (originally developed for optics) to **mechanical neural networks (MNNs)**. Shows that exact gradients can be obtained locally, enabling efficient training of mechanical systems for regression and classification tasks.

**Methodology:**
- **Theoretical:** Derives mechanical analogue of in situ backpropagation — exact gradient computation via local force/displacement measurements
- **Numerical validation:** Finite-element simulations of elastic beam networks
- **Tasks:** Regression, binary classification, multi-class classification
- **Retraining experiments:** Task-switching and damage recovery
- **Comparison:** Exact gradients vs. approximate gradients (finite differences)

**Key findings:**
- Exact local gradients enable efficient training of MNNs
- Successful training for behaviour learning and ML tasks with high accuracy
- MNNs are retrainable — can switch tasks and recover from structural damage
- Bridges the gap between optical PNNs (well-studied) and mechanical PNNs (nascent)

**Relevance to thesis:** Provides the **training methodology** for mechanical PNNs. The local gradient computation could be adapted to fluid systems — measuring local pressure gradients to update cylinder diameters in a vortex-street network.

### 12. Adamatzky (2019) — Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B
**Full title:** *The dry history of liquid computers*  
**Author:** Andrew Adamatzky  
**Venue:** Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 374, 20180372

**Main point:**  
Comprehensive historical survey of **liquid-based computing** from hydraulic calculators (17th century) through fluidic computers (1960s), microfluidics, droplet logic, and reaction-diffusion chemical computers. Establishes the long intellectual lineage of using fluids as computational substrates.

**Methodology:**
- **Historical taxonomy:** Chronological survey from 1900–2017
- **Categories:** Hydraulic calculators → fluidic logic gates → microfluidic devices → droplet computers → liquid marbles → reaction-diffusion chemical computers
- **Analysis:** Identifies recurring themes (mass-transfer analogies, flow-driven maze solving, chemical wave logic)

**Key findings:**
- Liquid computing has been reinvented multiple times across centuries
- Three roles of liquid: signal representation, mechanical actuation, chemical signal modification
- Reaction-diffusion chemical computers represent the most sophisticated liquid-computing paradigm
- Historical continuity supports the feasibility of fluid neural networks

**Relevance to thesis:** Provides the **historical and intellectual context** for fluid-based computation. Shows that using fluids for computing is not a new idea — the thesis contributes the wave-propagation and vortex-street variants to this lineage.

### 13. Adamatzky & De Lacy Costello (2002) — Phys. Rev. E
**Full title:** *Experimental logical gates in a reaction-diffusion medium: The XOR gate and beyond*  
**Authors:** Andrew Adamatzky & Benjamin De Lacy Costello  
**Venue:** Physical Review E, 66, 046112

**Main point:**  
Demonstrates **experimental construction of Boolean logic gates** (XOR, AND) in a reaction-diffusion chemical medium. Logic values are represented by the presence/absence of precipitate wave fronts; computation occurs through wave-front collisions in geometrically constrained gel substrates.

**Methodology:**
- **Chemical system:** Palladium chloride gel + potassium iodide reactant solution (or copper chloride + potassium hydroxide for AND gate)
- **Substrate:** Substrate-loaded gel with laser-cut or moulded channel geometry
- **Logic encoding:** Presence of precipitate = TRUE; absence = FALSE
- **Wiring:** Channels in gel act as "wires" for wave-front propagation
- **Readout:** Optical imaging of precipitate concentration
- **Operations:** XOR via wave-front annihilation; AND via constrained precipitation behind boundaries

**Key findings:**
- Laboratory prototypes of XOR and AND gates successfully demonstrated
- Three-valued composition derived from gate dynamics
- Wave-front interactions (annihilation, fusion, splitting) implement logical operations
- Computation is geometry-based, not kinetic-based (contrast with CSTR approaches)

**Relevance to thesis:** Seminal reference for **chemical computing via reaction-diffusion**. While the thesis has pivoted away from chemical systems, this paper establishes that non-electronic media can implement digital logic — supporting the broader argument that physical systems (including fluids) can compute.

### 14. Baltussen et al. (2024) — Nature
**Full title:** *Chemical reservoir computation in a self-organizing reaction network*  
**Authors:** Mathieu G. Baltussen, Thijs J. de Jong, Quentin Duez, William E. Robinson, Wilhelm T. S. Huck  
**Venue:** Nature, 631, 549–555

**Main point:**  
The **formose reaction** (a complex self-organising chemical reaction network) exhibits emergent reservoir-computing capabilities without bottom-up molecular engineering. It performs parallel nonlinear classification, dynamical system modelling, and time-series forecasting.

**Methodology:**
- **Chemical system:** Formose reaction in a continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR)
- **Inputs:** Time-varying concentrations of formaldehyde, NaOH, CaCl₂
- **Reservoir state:** Steady-state and transient chemical composition (monitored by online HPLC/MS)
- **Readout:** Linear regression from reservoir state to target
- **Tasks:**
  1. Parallel nonlinear classification (3 simultaneous tasks)
  2. Lorenz chaotic system forecasting
  3. Differential equation integration
- **Control:** Simple input parameters modulate complex self-organised response

**Key findings:**
- Complex chemical reaction networks can compute without molecule-by-molecule design
- Parallel task performance — one reservoir handles multiple independent computations
- Self-organisation provides nonlinearity and memory for reservoir computing
- Biomimetic approach: leverages emergent properties rather than imposing digital logic

**Relevance to thesis:** Demonstrates that **self-organising physical systems** (not just engineered ones) can compute. The formose reaction is a chemical analogue of the turbulent wake — both are complex, nonlinear, self-organising systems that can be harnessed for information processing.

### 15. Nagipogu & Reif (2025) — arXiv:2409.00034
**Full title:** *Neural CRNs: A Natural Implementation of Learning in Chemical Reaction Networks*  
**Authors:** Rajiv Teja Nagipogu & John H. Reif  
**Venue:** arXiv preprint

**Main point:**  
Proposes a **dynamical systems-based framework** for neural computation in chemical reaction networks (CRNs) where neural operations are modelled as time evolution of molecular concentrations. Achieves supervised learning with only two sequential phases and linear scalability.

**Methodology:**
- **Framework:** Neural computations as ODEs describing concentration dynamics (mass-action kinetics)
- **Circuit construction:**
  - Linear models: unimolecular reactions
  - Nonlinear models: bimolecular reactions (avoids higher-order chemistries)
- **Training:** Two-phase supervised learning (forward + weight update)
- **Gradient approximation:** First-order gradients natively incorporated into CRN framework
- **Validation:** Mass-action ODE simulations for regression and classification tasks

**Key findings:**
- End-to-end supervised learning with minimum two phases
- Linear scalability with input dimensionality (via first-order gradient approximations)
- Avoids complex higher-order chemical reactions — only unimolecular and bimolecular needed
- Validated across multiple regression and classification benchmarks

**Relevance to thesis:** Provides a **theoretical framework for chemical learning**. The CRN approach is more compact than digital emulation of neural networks. While chemically focused, the principle of encoding learning as continuous dynamical systems (ODEs) aligns with the wave-equation and Navier–Stokes approaches in the thesis.

### 16. Stovold & O'Keefe (2017) — Int. J. Parallel Emergent Distrib. Syst.
**Full title:** *Reaction–diffusion chemistry implementation of associative memory neural network*  
**Authors:** James Stovold & Simon O'Keefe  
**Venue:** International Journal of Parallel, Emergent and Distributed Systems, 32(1), 74–94

**Main point:**  
Implements **correlation matrix memories** (a form of associative memory neural network) in reaction-diffusion chemistry. Shows that unconventional computing substrates can be programmed using neural network architectures, bridging the programmability gap.

**Methodology:**
- **Neural model:** Correlation matrix memory (CMM) — outer-product associative memory
- **Chemical substrate:** Belousov-Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction in a structured gel
- **Encoding:** Binary patterns represented by chemical wave-front configurations
- **Training:** Hebbian-like learning through chemical wave-front superposition
- **Recall:** Present partial pattern → system evolves to complete stored pattern
- **Simulation:** Numerical integration of reaction-diffusion equations (Fitzhugh-Nagumo or Oregonator models)

**Key findings:**
- CMMs can be implemented in reaction-diffusion chemistry
- Associative memory functionality (pattern completion) achieved
- Neural network architectures provide a programmable interface for unconventional substrates
- Demonstrates robustness to noise and pattern corruption

**Relevance to thesis:** Shows that **associative memory** (a primitive neural function) can emerge from reaction-diffusion dynamics. The wave-front interaction mechanics are analogous to acoustic wave interference in diffractive neural networks.

### 17. Walter et al. (2023) — Nat. Commun.
**Full title:** *Real-time signal processing via chemical reactions for a microfluidic molecular communication system*  
**Authors:** Vivien Walter, Dadi Bi, Ali Salehi-Reyhani, Yansha Deng  
**Venue:** Nature Communications, 14, 6530

**Main point:**  
The **MIMIC platform** (Microfluidic Molecular Communication) performs complete digital signal processing chains — amplification, thresholding, shaping, detection — entirely through designed chemical reactions in flowing fluid. ASCII-encoded messages are transmitted as chemical bit sequences over metres of tubing.

**Methodology:**
- **Platform:** Continuous-flow microfluidic reactor with modular components
- **Signal representation:** NaOH concentration encodes binary bits
- **Processing primitives:**
  - **Amplification:** Autocatalytic reaction steps
  - **Thresholding:** pH-dependent colour change (indicator dyes)
  - **Shaping:** Controlled diffusion in microfluidic channels
  - **Detection:** Optical absorbance measurement
- **Transmission:** Chemical signals propagate through metres of PTFE tubing
- **Optimisation:** Data rate maximised while minimising bit-error rate

**Key findings:**
- Complete signal processing chain implemented chemically — no electronics
- Reliable transmission of ASCII text messages over long distances
- Real-time operation with modular, scalable architecture
- Demonstrates chemical circuits as viable I/O interfaces for molecular computing

**Relevance to thesis:** Provides an **engineering blueprint for chemical I/O interfaces**. While the thesis has pivoted from chemical to acoustic/fluid systems, the modular signal-processing approach (amplification → thresholding → detection) is directly analogous to how acoustic wave amplitudes could be read out in a fluid neural network.

## Methodology Glossary

| Term | Definition | Papers |
|---|---|---|
| **Reservoir Computing (RC)** | Framework where a fixed high-dimensional dynamical system (reservoir) is driven by inputs; only linear readout weights are trained | Goto 2021, Baltussen 2024, Jaafar 2022 |
| **Coupled Learning** | Contrastive learning: network run in free + clamped phases; local updates approximate global gradient | Stern 2021, Altman 2023 |
| **Physics-Aware Training (PAT)** | Hybrid algorithm: forward pass in physical system, backward pass through digital surrogate | Wright 2022 |
| **In situ Backpropagation** | Exact gradient measurement via local physical probes (e.g., force/displacement, intensity) | Li 2024, Hughes 2019 |
| **Correlation Matrix Memory (CMM)** | Outer-product associative memory; stored patterns are eigenvectors of correlation matrix | Stovold 2017 |
| **Formose Reaction** | Complex autocatalytic reaction network producing sugars from formaldehyde; exhibits self-organisation | Baltussen 2024 |
| **Soap-Film Tunnel** | Quasi-2D flow apparatus suppressing 3D turbulence; enables laminar vortex study over broad Re range | Kim 2017 |
| **FDTD / Finite-Difference** | Numerical discretisation of wave equation for time-domain simulation | Hughes 2019, Morast 2019 |
| **CSTR** | Continuous Stirred Tank Reactor — well-mixed flow reactor for maintaining steady-state chemistry | Baltussen 2024, Walter 2023 |
| **BZ Reaction** | Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction — oscillating chemical reaction exhibiting wave fronts and spirals | Adamatzky 2002, Stovold 2017 |
