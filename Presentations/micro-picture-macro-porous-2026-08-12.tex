We isolate each pore-scale element in structured 2D geometry and measure its transfer function:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Why 2D?} It is the controlled micro-scale limit. The same Oregonator kinetics govern 3D; 2D lets us isolate and calibrate the local rules before tackling a real pore network.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Practical reason for 2D first:} a projected light mask gives a known suppression field $\phi(x,y)$ everywhere in a thin slab. In a 3D bulk, absorption and scattering make the interior light field unknown, so the local kinetics would be uncontrolled.

\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{From measured operators to a graph simulator}

We translate the transfer functions into an event-driven pulse network:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nodes} = pores / junctions.
    \item \textbf{Edges} = throats with transit time $L/c$.
    \item \textbf{Refractory rule:} a node or edge that just fired ignores new pulses for $\tau \approx 3.0$ t.u.
    \item \textbf{Inhibition rule:} two pulses arriving at the same node within $\approx 1.0$ t.u. annihilate.
    \item \textbf{Routing table:} junction topology decides which input reaches which output.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Value:} a fast design tool. We can explore candidate pore networks in milliseconds before committing to a full PDE simulation.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Geometry matters: when the ideal rule fails}

A symmetric Y-junction with a chamber should give XOR, but the PDE shows a leak:

\vspace{0.3cm}
\begin{table}
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Pattern} & \textbf{Graph prediction} & \textbf{PDE (chamber $R = 25$)} \\
\midrule
01 / 10 & output fires & output fires \\
11 & no output & \textbf{output still fires} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Why:} extended channel fronts collide, but the excitation curves around the annihilation point and re-ignites the output stem.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Learning:} inhibition rules are geometry-dependent. The graph model must be PDE-calibrated per junction shape, not assumed universal.

\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What this contributes}

\textbf{Method:} a workflow to extract discrete event rules from continuous Oregonator PDEs and compose them into a fast network simulator.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Data:} calibrated local operators for pulse conduction, filtering, and inhibition in a structured BZ medium.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Finding:} network-level pulse routing can be predicted from micro-scale transfer functions, but only if junction geometry is accounted for.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Path forward:} use the measured operators to inform effective porous-medium equations and design structured chemical reactors / molecular-communication substrates.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{From 2D to 3D:} the graph framework itself is dimensionless. Extending to 3D requires (i) a volumetric light-delivery strategy (the current blocker), and (ii) recalibrating the local operators in 3D throats. Once those are solved, the same network simulator applies.

\vspace{0.3cm}
\textbf{Qualified claim:} our 2D measurements characterise the local reaction--diffusion operators that govern pulse traffic in a 3D porous BZ reactor. They do not capture 3D curvature, random tortuosity, or the full connectivity of a real pore network.

\end{frame}
