\begin{block}{Motivation}
Digital inference is limited by the von Neumann bottleneck. Physical neural networks use the material itself as the computer: inputs are excitations, structure is the weights, and outputs are probe readings. We treat a structured Belousov--Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction--diffusion reactor as a porous computing substrate.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Porous reactor picture}
A macroscopic porous block is a network of pores (nodes) connected by throats (edges). Full 3D continuum simulation is too expensive. Instead, we abstract the geometry as a graph and calibrate each element with controlled 2D simulations.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Model and solver}
The two-variable Tyson--Fife Oregonator with photosensitive suppression:
\begin{align*}
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} &= \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left[u-u^2-(fv+\phi)\frac{u-q}{u+q}\right]+\nabla\!\cdot\!(\mathbf{D}\nabla u),\\
\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} &= u-v.
\end{align*}
An explicit finite-difference solver with operator splitting and adaptive reaction subcycling is verified against manufactured solutions.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Measured pore-scale operators}
Four native operators are isolated with a black-box dark-spot protocol.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{1. Wire}
A straight throat transmits a pulse at the kinetic speed with almost no width dependence.
\begin{itemize}
\item 2D channel speed: 6.46 cells/t.u.
\item 3D channel speed: 6.62 cells/t.u.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{../Analysis/figures/pub/pub_channel_transfer_darkspot.png}
\caption{Channel pulse transfer: speed is independent of channel width.}
\end{figure}

\begin{block}{2. Low-pass filter}
Refractoriness sets the maximum signalling frequency. Absolute refractory period: 3.0 t.u.; max 1:1 following rate: 0.167 t.u.$^{-1}$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{3. Inhibition gate}
At a T-junction, a pulse on one arm blocks the other within a short window. A AND (NOT B) is obtained with separation $>200\times$.
\end{block}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\columnwidth]{../Analysis/figures/pub/pub_tjunction_logic_darkspot.png}

\end{figure}

\begin{block}{4. Anisotropic routing}
A tilted diffusion tensor steers pulses; the fast-to-slow speed ratio follows the eikonal $\sqrt{r}$ law.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Graph simulator}
The measured operators become an event-driven graph simulator: edges carry transit times, nodes carry refractory and inhibition windows. It predicts pulse traffic before an expensive PDE run.
\end{block}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\columnwidth]{../Analysis/figures/rd_multi_gate_pde_snapshots.png}

\begin{block}{Boundary experiments}
\begin{itemize}
\item 3D inhibition gate transfers cleanly: 16.8$\times$ separation, A arrival 8.25 t.u.
\item Collision XOR leaks in free medium and in a simple chamber; junction geometry must be calibrated.
\item A simple one-way diode is not robust in this regime.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Conclusions}
Network-level pulse routing in a structured excitable medium can be predicted from micro-scale transfer functions, but only when each junction geometry is carried explicitly. Next steps: a shape-resolved junction library, volumetric 3D illumination, and experimental calibration.
\end{block}
